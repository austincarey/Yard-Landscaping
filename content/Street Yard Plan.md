# Street Yard Plan: Turf to Native Garden Conversion

This document outlines the step-by-step process for converting the front turfgrass street lawn into a native perennial, shrub, and groundcover garden, based on research from [[Street Lawn Research]].

## Phase 1: Grass Eradication (Herbicide-Free)
**Method: Sheet Mulching (Lasagna Composting)**
Yes, covering with cardboard, wetting it, and mulching is the absolute best, herbicide-free method to eradicate turfgrass. It improves your soil while killing the grass!
1. **Mow Low**: Mow the existing grass as low as your mower can go.
2. **Cardboard Layer**: Lay down plain, non-glossy cardboard (remove all tape/staples) or thick layers of newspaper. **Crucial:** Overlap the edges by at least 6-8 inches so no light gets through. Grass will aggressively find the seams.
3. **Wet it Down**: Thoroughly soak the cardboard to jumpstart decomposition and keep it securely in place.
4. **Compost Layer**: Add 3 inches of Bio 1 compost directly on top of the cardboard. This will rapidly speed up soil improvement and provide a rich base for the heavy Longmont clay.
5. **Mulch Layer**: Cover the compost with 3 inches of your chosen long-term mulch.

### Recommended Long-Term Mulch Options
Given the Longmont climate and the native plant selection, here are the best long-term options:
- **Arborist Wood Chips (Ramial Chipped Wood)**: *(Top Recommendation)* Often free from local arborists (via services like ChipDrop). The varying sizes of wood, twigs, and leaves interlock well, making them resistant to wind. They break down at an ideal rate to feed the clay soil and fungal networks over several years without needing constant replenishment.
- **Shredded Western Red Cedar / "Gorilla Hair"**: The fibrous texture mats down and interlocks tightly, making it almost impervious to wind and heavy rain washout. It is the longest-lasting wood mulch. *Note: It can become water-repellent if it dries out completely, and is more flammable than chunky wood chips.*
- **Pine Needles (Pine Straw)**: Since you are planting under a mature pine, utilizing its natural needle drop is highly recommended. Pine straw interlocks perfectly, breathes well (allowing water to infiltrate easily), and looks incredibly natural for a native understory garden.

*Recommendation*: A base of **Arborist Wood Chips** is generally the best all-around choice for a new sheet-mulch project because of its superior soil-building properties and cost-effectiveness. You can let the natural pine needles fall over it as time goes on.

## Phase 2: Irrigation Conversion
**Method: Retrofitting Sprinklers to Drip**
You do *not* need to dig up the entire system or lay completely new underground PVC lines.
1. **Sprinkler Conversion Kits**: You can purchase multi-port drip manifolds (e.g., Rain Bird or Orbit) that screw directly onto the existing 1/2-inch underground risers of your rotary sprinklers once you unscrew the heads.
2. **Capping**: Convert 1 or 2 strategically located sprinkler heads per zone into these drip manifolds. Cap off the remaining unused rotary heads in each zone using cheap PVC caps.
3. **Laying the Drip Line (Do NOT bury it!)**:
   - Drip tubing (the 1/2" main poly line and 1/4" emitter lines) should be laid directly on the soil/cardboard surface, **underneath** the mulch layer. 
   - Burying it makes it extremely prone to clogging with dirt and roots, and impossible to troubleshoot or repair. The mulch will protect the plastic tubing from UV degradation and hide it aesthetically.
   - For a dense garden, consider running blank 1/2" main lines and punching in 1/4" emitter lines pointing directly to the root zones of specific shrubs and plant clusters.

## Phase 3: Plant Layout & Phasing
*(Referencing [[Street Lawn Research]] for species selection)*
- **The "Bones" (Shrubs)**: Start by placing your structural plants first. These provide winter interest and scale. Consider [[Serviceberry]], [[Boulder Raspberry]], or [[Golden Currant]]. 
- **The "Drifts" (Perennials)**: Plant groups of 3-5 of the same perennials together (e.g., [[Blue Mist Penstemon]], [[Blanket Flower]], [[Wild Bergamot]]) for visual impact and to make it easier for pollinators to forage.
- **The "Living Mulch" (Groundcovers)**: Plant [[Kinnikinnick]] and [[Creeping Oregon Grape]] between the larger plants. Over 2-3 years, these will spread, creating a living green mat that suppresses weeds and reduces the need to reapply wood mulch.
- **How to Plant**: When planting time comes, just push aside the mulch, cut an "X" in the cardboard, dig a small hole for your 2-inch plug or potted plant, nestle it in, and push the mulch back around the base.

## Additional Considerations
1. **Edge Definition**: Without grass roots to hold it, heavy rain can wash your new mulch onto the sidewalk. Plan for a hard edge (like steel edging, small boulders, or a 4-inch deep "V" trench) along the concrete perimeter.
2. **Soil Amendment**: Try to avoid tilling the soil, especially if working near the mature pine tree mentioned in your research. Let the sheet mulching break down the clay organically. When digging holes for individual plants, mix a handful of compost in with the native clay.
3. **HOA & City Regulations**: Check Longmont city codes or HOA rules regarding the percentage of vegetative cover required in a street yard (often there are rules stating 50%+ must be living plant material at maturity) and height restrictions for plants near intersections/driveways.
4. **The "Sleep, Creep, Leap" Phase**: Native plants spend their first year growing roots (sleep), their second year slowly spreading (creep), and their third year exploding in growth (leap). The first 1-2 years will require diligent weeding as the native groundcovers fill in.
